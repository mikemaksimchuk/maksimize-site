1️⃣Chapter 1 – What Is “Instructionally Useful”Chapter 1 – What Is “Instructionally Useful” Assessment?
Overview of Chapter 1
● The authors introduce the concept of “instructionally useful” assessment, distinguishing it from
assessments that merely measure outcomes without informing next steps.
● Key ideas include: purpose of assessment, alignment with instructional goals, and the impact on both
teaching and learning.
Discussion Prompts
● Defining Utility (p. 21): Evans and Marion discuss the importance of assessments that ‘change what
happens the next day in the classroom.’ What examples come to mind from your own practice?
● Shifting Mindset (p. 6): “The authors posit that a major shift in mindset occurs when we see assessments
as integral to the teaching process rather than an endpoint. How does this mindset shift affect the way
you plan lessons or units?”
● Assessments for Learning vs. Assessments of Learning (pp. 12-15): How do we balance the need to
measure students’ mastery with the desire to use assessments formatively?
● How are standards turned into learning targets? How are these learning targets used to develop
assessments? How do you ensure that your assessments are at an appropriate depth of knowledge?
● Coherence between standards, curriculum, and assessments: How is this reviewed in your district? How
can you continue to work towards coherence?
Application Activity & Preview
● Classroom/School Audit: Invite participants to review one recent assessment they’ve used. Have them
identify one aspect that aligns well with the concept of instructional usefulness and one aspect that needs
improvement. They can share a brief summary of their findings at the next session.
2️⃣Chapter 2 – Features that InfluenceChapter 2 – Features that Influence Instructional Utility
Overview of Chapter 2
● Explores the specific design and implementation features that either enhance or hinder the instructional
usefulness of assessments.
● Focus on validity, reliability, timeliness of feedback, and alignment with learning targets.
Discussion Prompts
● What are your thoughts on the three pathways of using data from an assessment (page 32)
○ How do you change the mindsets of colleagues who do not see the need to analyze their testing
data and make changes?
● Examine the Ten Features of Assessment Design for Utility that make an assessment instructionally
useful. What are your thoughts about these features?
● Complexity & Associated Item Types (Feature #1): There is a difference between difficulty and
complexity, the depth of knowledge, the content type, and how it should be assessed. How do we make
sure teachers have this assessment literacy and knowledge? Especially this day & age of teachers
sometimes having alternative pathways?
○ Review the Michigan Assessment Consortium (MAC)’s Assessment Literacy Standards
● Timeliness & Feedback (pp. 50-51): Evans and Marion emphasize that delayed feedback diminishes an
assessment’s usefulness. How have you seen feedback timing impact student outcomes?
● Clarity of Learning Targets (pp. 35–47): On these pages, the authors don’t explicitly state it, but they
discuss the necessity of clear learning targets for both teachers and students. How do you currently
communicate learning targets in your context?
● Real world & cultural connections: How do you make these connections and still ensure that the
assessment is equitable for all students, regardless of background?
● Practical Constraints: What real-world constraints—time, resources, district policies—do you face
when trying to design or select instructionally useful assessments, based on these 10 features? What are
some possible solutions to these problems of practice?
Application Activity & Preview
● Assessment Feature Check: Choose or bring an assessment from your district or school. Using a
simple checklist of the Ten Features of Assessment Design for Utility discussed in Chapter 2,
evaluate how well the assessment meets each feature. Note at least one change you could make to
improve it.

● Using a simple checklist of the Ten Features of Assessment Design for Utility discussed in Chapter

improve it. Any insights?
○ Is there a way to hit all 10 of these features? Student-centered, project-based,

Overview of Chapter 3
● Delves into the critical role of formative assessments in shaping day-to-day teaching and learning.
● Highlights how ongoing checks for understanding, real-time feedback, and student involvement can
drive learning progress.
Discussion Prompts
● Defining Formative (pp. 68-79): In your own words, how do you differentiate ‘formative assessment’
from other types? How does your definition align with that of the authors?
○ Formative assessment is not a thing - but a process
○ MAC Learning Point: The difference an S can make (reference page 69 of text)
● Can a summative assessment be used formatively? What is formative about it?
● Evans and Marion emphasize involving students in this formative assessment process. What strategies
have you used, or could you use, to help students become active assessors of their own learning?”
● How could you inject student choice and voice (i.e. Myron Dueck’s book) into the formative assessment
process?
● Informing Instruction (p. 91) “Have you experienced a scenario where formative assessments made you
significantly change your lesson plans or approach for the next day?”
● Is there a way to use technology to provide effective, immediate feedback? (AI ethics?)
● Grading/Reporting: Should formative assessment be entered in the gradebook? If so, how? If not, why?
Application Activity & Preview
● Try a New Technique: Implement a new formative assessment technique (e.g., exit tickets,
think-pair-share, or quick polls)in your classroom - or encourage teachers in your building to implement
a new technique. Collect anecdotal feedback on how it influenced their instructional decisions. Share
experiences in the next session.
● Identify the standard, depth of knowledge, analysis methods used, and how students will receive
feedback on their learning. How are other stakeholders involved in this process? Are results/feedback
shared with parents/guardians as well for additional support at home?
4️⃣Chapter 4 – Local District SummativeChapter 4 – Local District Summative & Interim Assessments
Overview of Chapter 4
● Examines how district-level summative and interim assessments can (and should) be designed or
interpreted to support instruction, not just accountability.
● Considers how data from these assessments can feed back into curriculum planning and professional
development.
Discussion Prompts
● Based on Ten Features of Assessment Design for Utility summative and interim assessments are less
useful for teachers to make changes to the curriculum for their current students. How should teachers
use summative assessments to adjust learning for future units - and the curriculum for future years’
students?
● Can a summative assessment be used formatively? Could a student use this assessment and receive
feedback, close any gaps in their learning, and then re-assess? Should students be allowed to re-assess
on summative assessments?
● Discuss a time when either summative or interim assessment data influenced your instructional
decisions. Was it for the group of students in front of you - or was it for the next years’ students?
● Interim vs. Benchmark vs. Summative How do the authors differentiate interim assessments from
summative assessments? Do you see this distinction in your district’s assessment calendar/system?
● Data Interpretation (pp. 112-115): What protocols do you have in place to interpret and act on district
assessment data? What additional strategies might you consider, based on the authors’ suggestions?
● Building Collaborative Culture (pp. 117-118): The authors note that local assessments are most powerful
when interpreted collaboratively. How do you foster a data-focused collaborative culture in your setting?
Application Activity & Preview
● District Data Culture: Reflect on your school or district’s current data culture. What collaborative
structures are already in place for interpreting data? What barriers exist? What is one new strategy you
could try (e.g., data PLCs, teacher-led discussions, student-involved data analysis)? Develop an idea to
bring back to district leadership & share.
● Interim/Summative Utility Audit: Using the "Ten Features of Assessment Design for Utility," review a
district-level interim or summative assessment you use. How instructionally useful is this assessment?
What changes could make it more useful for informing instruction? Jot down 2-3 recommendations for
improvement to bring back to district teams.
5️⃣Chapter 5 – State AssessmentsChapter 5 – The Instructional Usefulness of State Assessments
Overview of Chapter 5
● Addresses the challenges and opportunities of state-level assessments.
● Outlines methods for interpreting large-scale data and using results in a way that benefits local
instruction and improvement efforts.
Discussion Prompts
● Limitations & Opportunities: The authors discuss how state assessments have limitations due to their
design, scope, and frequency. Despite these limits, where do you see the possibility of instructional
improvement based on state data?
○ How might/should(?) state assessment data drive changes to curricula or classroom instruction?
○ Is there a culture in place that allows for staff to be comfortable to dissect this data and reflect on
their own teaching practices?
○ Is the data analysis easy to perform? Are there roadblocks? How can you guide teachers down
the path of data analysis?
● Equity Considerations (pp. 142-144): How can state assessment data shed light on equity issues within a
district or school? How might you address those issues?
● Communicating Results (p. 146-148): Evans and Marion talk about effectively communicating state
assessment results to stakeholders. What strategies have worked (or failed) in your experience?
Application Activity & Preview
● Communicating Results: Review pages 146-148 on effective communication of assessment results.
Reflect on what has worked (or failed) when discussing state data with staff, parents, or the community?
How can data storytelling make the results more meaningful? Develop a one-minute “data message” that
could be shared with stakeholders (e.g., teachers, parents, school board). Include the assessment, the
trend, the topic, and next steps.
● Overcoming Roadblocks: Reflect on your state data analysis process. Is state data easily accessible and
understandable for teachers? What roadblocks exist (e.g., data format, complexity, lack of training)?
How can administrators and leaders support teachers in using data effectively? How could long-term
trends influence curriculum adjustments? Brainstorm one simple way your district could make state data
analysis easier or more actionable for teachers.

● Job-Embedded PD (p. 164-166): The authors emphasize the value of job-embedded professional

rather than a one-time event, and that this learning is sustained?
Application Activity & Preview

🏁 Final Reflection & Next StepsFinal Reflection and Action Plan
Synthesis
Instructionally useful assessment is defined not by how often we assess, but by how effectively we use
assessment evidence to inform teaching and support student learning. Assessments become meaningful when
they are intentionally designed to align with learning goals, provide clear evidence of student understanding,
and guide instructional decisions in real time.
This work emphasizes that assessment is not separate from instruction—it is an integral part of the teaching and
learning process. When educators use assessment evidence to identify where students are in their learning, they
are better positioned to adjust instruction, provide targeted support, and extend learning opportunities. At the
same time, students benefit when assessment practices help them understand their progress and next steps.
Ultimately, instructionally useful assessment requires thoughtful design, careful interpretation of evidence, and
a commitment to using that evidence to improve learning outcomes for all students.

or increased student engagement.
