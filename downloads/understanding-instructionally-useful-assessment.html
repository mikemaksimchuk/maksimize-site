Understanding Instructionally Useful Assessment

Book author(s): Carla Evans & Scott Marion

HTML reading edition of Mike’s March 2026 study guide, with updated contact details. This preserves the extracted text, not the original PDF design. Historical credit instructions below do not confirm current SCECH availability. Contact Mike about current options.

By Carla Evans, PhD and Scott Marion, PhD Thank you for joining us on this journey! This study plan is designed for educators to delve into the principles and practices discussed in "Understanding Instructionally Useful Assessment." The plan spans the six chapters, with each chapter dedicated to a specific area of focus. Participants will engage in reading, self-reflection, and application activities to deepen their understanding and prepare for implementation of the concepts presented. The following session outline encourages practical application of the concepts in the text, as well as self-reflection on current practices within the district. Remember to take notes as you read, and reflect on how the information can be applied to your individual district or setting. Participants are encouraged to engage in discussions with peers or colleagues to further enrich their understanding, or engage colleagues in the suggested activities. Synthesis

Instructionally useful assessment is defined not by how often we assess, but by how effectively we use assessment evidence to inform teaching and support student learning. Assessments become meaningful when they are intentionally designed to align with learning goals, provide clear evidence of student understanding, and guide instructional decisions in real time. This work emphasizes that assessment is not separate from instruction—it is an integral part of the teaching and learning process. When educators use assessment evidence to identify where students are in their learning, they are better positioned to adjust instruction, provide targeted support, and extend learning opportunities. At the same time, students benefit when assessment practices help them understand their progress and next steps. Ultimately, instructionally useful assessment requires thoughtful design, careful interpretation of evidence, and a commitment to using that evidence to improve learning outcomes for all students.

Personal Reflection and Action Plan

  • What are my top three takeaways from this book study? Reflect on the ideas or practices that most influenced your thinking about assessment and its role in instruction.
  • How has this book study influenced my understanding of the relationship between assessment and instruction? Consider how your current practices align with the idea of assessment as a tool for guiding teaching and learning.
  • What is one specific change I will make to ensure my assessments are more instructionally useful? Identify a practical step you can take to improve how you design, use, or respond to assessment evidence.
  • What evidence from my current context suggests that this change is needed? Reflect on examples such as student work, assessment results, or instructional challenges that highlight opportunities for improvement.
  • How will I use assessment evidence more effectively to inform instructional decisions? Consider how you might adjust lessons, provide feedback, or differentiate instruction based on what you learn from assessments.
  • Who will support or hold me accountable for implementing this change? Identify colleagues, mentors, or professional learning communities that can support your reflection and growth.
  • What is my timeline for implementing and reviewing this change? Outline when you will implement the change and when you will evaluate its impact.
  • How will I know if this change improves student learning and instructional effectiveness? Identify the types of evidence you will look for, such as improved student understanding, more targeted instruction, or increased student engagement.
  • What additional support or learning do I need to continue strengthening my assessment practices?
